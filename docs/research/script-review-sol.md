# Spoken-script review

These mostly sound like credible human coaches. The core instructions are plain, usable and restrained. Reps is the most convincing overall; Land also has a believable, unhurried voice. Box and Cool-down become more written-sounding in their debriefs. Turn the Tide is the least natural: several stretches sound like a carefully compressed science explainer dropped into a breathing session. Clear Nose is mostly direct and practical.

There is a noticeable Claude-ish editorial finish in places, but the text alone cannot establish who wrote it. The tell is how often a useful cue gets followed by a perfectly packaged little takeaway. Leo and Mira fit the requested tone, though they share so many sentence habits that they sometimes sound like one writer in two voices. That doesn't justify changing otherwise good lines.

The specific tics are:

- **Fragment stacking:** “Shoulders, jaw, hands, head.” and “Heart rate, breath, head.” sound like a checklist being read. “Your chest and upper back. The breath moving in there.” needs an actual invitation to notice. By contrast, “Belly, then ribs, then chest” gives an ordered action, and the short anatomical labels in a body scan earn their place.
- **Aphorism closers:** “An even box beats a perfect one,” “Both count,” and “Steady is better than fast” turn simple instructions into little maxims. A real coach could say any one of them; their accumulation makes the writing feel manufactured.
- **Repeated ‘That's the X’ payoffs:** “That's the lever you're pulling,” “that's your benchmark,” and “That's where the training actually lands” keep converting the experience into a neat lesson. “That's a rep” earns its place because it explains the exercise. “That's the cool-down” is also an ordinary closing label, not automatically a problem.
- **Too-neat contrast and parallelism:** “a quiet day … a loud one” sounds like copy. “Slower air, not more air” is less natural than a direct correction. The four box phases, counted ratios, and genuine lists of options should stay parallel; listeners need that structure.
- **Rule-of-three rhythm:** “Water, food, sleep” gets followed by a polished recovery metaphor. That combination feels composed. Three body parts, three alternatives, or three study outcomes are not a problem just because there are three of them.
- **Phrases that are harder to imagine aloud:** “Let it breathe itself,” “some days are busy in the head,” “Resting, not straining,” and “your breathing can reach your immune system” are compressed or indirect. “Properly ill” also sits awkwardly in the requested American voice.
- **Assumed experiences:** “Notice how heavy your legs are,” “Notice what came down,” and “that's what makes you tingly and light” preselect the listener's result. The replacements make room for those sensations without requiring them.

The timing reinforces the difference. A short label followed by a long gap works well. Turn the Tide's `tw_co2` has 73 words between 6:44 and the 7:18 cue; `tw_kox` has 99 between 10:06 and 10:51. Those require roughly 129 and 132 words per minute respectively, before allowing breathing room. They can fit, but they are substantial listening tasks during recovery. The edits below shorten them while preserving the explanations and caveats. I would not relocate them or delete their substance under this brief.

For the science wording, the [box-breathing trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/) supports retaining the study result while avoiding a settled causal explanation. The [humming experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12119224/) and [combined-training experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24799686/) support keeping the study-specific limits rather than turning them into treatment promises. The stress/sleep-to-colds wording draws on [stress research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1713648/) and [sleep research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26118561/); the superiority of tonight's rest over this session is an inference, so the replacement makes that comparison tentative. The delayed breath-urge explanation retains the caution described in this [breath-hold physiology explanation](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/articles/shallow-water-hypoxic-blackout).

One instruction inconsistency is worth resolving separately: `cl_how` says “When you really need to breathe,” while round one's timed call says “the first clear urge.” I have kept both thresholds as written rather than choosing a different instruction in a voice review.
