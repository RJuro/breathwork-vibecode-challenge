Compiled 9 Oct 2026. Everything is paraphrased. Timings and protocols are stated in full because they are
facts; quotes are rare and 10 words or fewer.

Method
- First mined the earlier transcripts (genre-research/transcripts, breathwrk-research/transcripts).
- Then ran 29 YouTube searches on the Spark and transcribed 37 more videos with Parakeet (timestamps).
  Transcripts are in sequences-yt/transcripts/<id>.txt|json. Search hits are in search1.txt and search2.txt.
- Phase lengths were measured from cue timestamps (bin/seg.py prints segments with gaps; small Python
  one-offs measured cue-to-cue intervals). "Measured" below means taken from the audio; "stated" means the
  presenter's own number. They often differ: counted seconds usually run slow.
- Dropped: 8vkYJf8DOsc (panic video, music only, no speech); hmq7ySQZ5w8 (download failed); the two
  rest-step videos z9tVwAxCO6s and H1LzSF8uFJw (about leg mechanics, no breathing timings).

Evidence labels used in "My judgement": SOLID / SOME / SPECULATIVE / WRONG (garbled science in the video).
Kumbha fit labels: NEW practice / VARIANT of an existing one / ALREADY COVERED / SKIP.
Voice-line estimates count new recorded lines (line() and tip()); count clips made with say() are listed
separately where they matter. Reuse of existing lines (T.*, L.*) is assumed wherever it fits.

Side note from reading the flows: clear.js's on-screen lede names a real teacher ("Patrick McKeown's
nose-unblocking exercise"), and tide.js's notes do too. That conflicts with the no-real-names rule. Citations
in the Sources lists are probably fine; the lede probably isn't.

1. BOX BREATHING WITH A TACTICAL OPENER  ("Box")
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: stay level-headed before or after something stressful (a call-out, a hard meeting, an exam).
Train it when calm so it is there under pressure.

Structure, as the videos run it
- C2, two drills.
  - Drill 1, the tactical breath: 4-count inhale (belly, then lower ribs, then chest), 8-count exhale.
    Explained at 4:27-6:20. He also allows 4:4 or 4:6. A slight pause at the top is fine.
  - Drill 2, box: stated 5-5-5-5 (in, hold, out, hold) for about 5 minutes, timed by an app's tones.
    The guided set runs 9:41-14:45.
    - Measured: 9 inhale cues, median cycle 27.6 s, so about 6.9 s per side and 2.2 breaths a minute.
      He says "three breaths per minute", which would be 5 s per side. The app counted slow.
    - About 10 cycles in 5 minutes. It starts with a full exhale (9:36).
- N4, the counted version: stated 4-4-4-4 with numbers spoken at about 1.0 s each. Measured cycle 16-17 s
  (0:39-1:29), i.e. about 3.6 breaths a minute. Counts for 4 cycles, then single-word cues only, then
  bells. 5:45 in all.
- N15 includes 4 box rounds at about 4-4-4-4 (measured 15-16 s per round, 2:27-3:29).
- N1 (hospital): in, hold 1-5 s, out, hold 1-5 s, repeat 7-10 times. Sitting with feet grounded helps,
  but it can be done anywhere.
- N2 (police): 4-4-4-4 "on repeat", used on the way to a hard call or after it.

Proposed Kumbha structure (Standard), about 7 minutes
- Arrive, 30 s: the plan in numbers.
- Tactical breath, 4 in : 8 out, 6 breaths (72 s). Belly, ribs, chest on the way in; throat slightly
  narrowed on the way out.
- Box 4-4-4-4, 8 rounds (2:08).
- Box 5-5-5-5, 6 rounds (2:00).
- Close, 30 s: the benchmark check.
- Gentle: 3-3-3-3 then 4-4-4-4, holds soft. Deeper: 5-5-5-5 then 6-6-6-6 (24-s cycles, 2.5 a minute).
- No holds: run the tactical 4:8 throughout. This is the honest fallback, since the evidence below does not
  favour the holds anyway.
- Engine: pace {inhale, holdIn, exhale, holdOut} already exists, as used by 4-7-8 and HUM.

Cues that made it work
- C2 states the plan as two drills before starting, and names the 1:2 ratio as "one-two": four in is the
  one, eight out is the two (5:53).
- Explains when each drill is used: the 4:8 is for performing, under pressure; box is for training in a
  quiet room so the pattern is there when needed (6:20-8:05).
- Once running, nearly every cue is one word: inhale, hold, exhale, hold (from 9:45). Extra lines are rare
  and practical: soften your gaze or close your eyes; relax; clear your mind.
- A "few more rounds" call near the end (13:16), then "one more" (14:10).
- The close is a benchmark: notice how your body and head feel, compare with the start, note it (14:46).
  That suits the coach voice exactly.
- N2's practical framing: do it on the way to a hard call, and nobody knows you're doing it.
- N4's reassurance early on: don't worry about being exact.

Safety
- Seated or standing still. Not while driving, despite N2's "on the way to a call" (fine for a passenger).
- The holds are short (4-6 s), so blackout risk is negligible. Still skip the holds if pregnant, or if
  holding makes you anxious; use the 4:8 instead.
- Don't push the counts up into strain; 6-6-6-6 is a sensible ceiling.

Evidence
- As presented:
  - C2: calmer and clearer thinking under duress. The science is garbled: he says nasal breathing
    releases "nitrous oxide", which then delivers oxygen and nitrogen.
  - N1: lowers blood pressure and heart rate, improves mood.
  - N2: keeps you out of fight-or-flight, so you respond rather than react.
- My judgement:
  - SOME. Slow breathing at 3-6 a minute reliably shifts heart rate and HRV.
  - In the 2023 one-month remote trial Kumbha already cites for cyclic sighing, a 5-5-5-5 box arm also
    lowered anxiety and improved mood, but less than sighing on positive mood.
  - I'm not aware of evidence that the holds add anything over a plain long exhale. I recall a 2021
    comparison of box-style "combat tactical breathing" with prolonged exhalation that found no clear edge
    for the box (Röttger et al. 2021); verify before citing.
  - Its real strengths are that it's easy to remember, easy to count, and widely taught to police and
    military.

Kumbha fit
- NEW practice under Calm, or a new "Focus" tag.
- The best fit for the ex-military coach in the whole set: drills, ratios, benchmark. It is the most
  obvious gap in the library.
- Voice: about 12-14 new lines: welcome, plan, three-part breath, throat, when-to-use, box intro, soft
  hold, gaze, science, where it's used, benchmark close, plus 2-3 tips.
- Count clips: "In. Hold. Out. Hold." (4, shared) and "Six breaths, four in, eight out." style
  announcements.

2. LONG-EXHALE LADDER  (2:4, 3:6, 4:8)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: bring yourself down fast when anxious or wound up. It's the simplest panic tool there is.

Structure
- N5: in through the nose for 3, out through the mouth for 6, counted. The counts run at about 1 s each:
  "in" at 1:18.8, "out" at 1:24.9, the next "in" at 1:31.7, so a 12.9-s cycle and 4.7 breaths a minute.
  - Only 3 counted breaths, then free breathing.
  - Scaling is offered up front (0:45): if 3:6 is too long, use 2:4; to go further, 4:8.
- C2's tactical breath is the same idea at 4:8.
- N15 opens with 6 breaths at about 4:8 (measured 4.1 s in, 12.0-s cycle).
- N28 (see 17) runs 4:8 for about 4 minutes, lying down.

Proposed: start at 2:4 for 4 breaths, then 3:6 for 6, then 4:8 for 8. About 3 minutes.

Cues that made it work
- The scaling is offered before starting, so nobody fails the count.
- Breathe into the belly on the way in, out through the mouth.
- A closing "notice how the mind and body feel now".

Safety
- Very low risk. If light-headed, make the breaths smaller rather than longer.
- For a first-ever panic attack, or chest pain, seek medical help rather than breathing through it.

Evidence
- As presented: slows your breathing, heart rate "and even your thoughts" (N5, 0:10).
- My judgement:
  - SOME for the acute calming effect: exhale-weighted slow breathing raises vagal HRV.
  - Kumbha already cites Meehan & Shaffer 2024: at 6 breaths a minute, 1:1 and 1:2 ratios did about the
    same. It is the slowing that matters most.

Kumbha fit
- Mostly covered already: Wind-down has 4:6 into 4:8, and Window Seat has 4:8.
- Best used as the second half of the "Steady" practice in entry 3, or as the no-holds track of "Box".
- Voice: 3-4 lines if folded into another practice.

3. SMALL HOLDS  ("Steady": many small breath holds)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: settle over-breathing, a panic spike, a tickly cough, or "can't get a satisfying breath". It is the
opposite of a sigh: it builds a little CO2 back up instead of blowing it off.

Structure, N6, measured
- Setup: sit upright, mouth closed, tongue resting on the roof of the mouth, nose only throughout.
- Each cycle:
  - a normal, small, quiet breath in and out through the nose;
  - pinch the nose and hold, while the coach counts 5-4-3-2-1. Measured hold 5.2-6.2 s (9 holds);
  - let go of the nose and breathe normally, mouth closed, for the stated 10-15 s. Measured cycle median
    29 s (range 24-36 s), which includes the pre-hold breath.
- 10 holds from 1:21 to 5:49, about 4.5 minutes, then 30 s of sitting quietly.
- Stated: holds of 3-5 s, rests of 10-15 s, for 5-10 minutes or until symptoms settle (0:45).

Proposed Kumbha structure
- 1 min of nose-only settling.
- 10-12 cycles: small breath in and out, hold 5 s on the exhale (Gentle 3 s; Deeper 7 s), rest 12 s.
- 2 min of small, quiet breathing, or the 3:6 ladder from entry 2.
- Close. About 7 minutes in all.
- Engine: clear-nose's pinch() step (hold: 'empty', label 'Pinch & nod') plus { rest: 12 }. Nothing new is
  needed.

Cues that made it work
- Every hold starts from a normal small breath, not a deep one. The video repeats this, because people
  instinctively take a big breath first (1:35).
- The count-down in the hold makes it feel short and certain.
- Between holds: shoulders soften, belly loose, lips sealed (2:03-2:35).
- Expect a pull to take a deeper breath at some point; let it pass and stay small (4:04).
- Name the possible "positive signs" as allowed, not promised: extra saliva, warmth (4:36).
- Ends with "use it any time, or as and when you need it".

Safety
- The video itself says no breath holds if pregnant, and to check with a practitioner if you have a serious
  condition.
- Kumbha's standard safety block applies. Holds of 5 s carry essentially no blackout risk.
- Do not use it to self-manage chest pain, a first-ever attack, or an asthma attack that isn't settling
  with your reliever: get help.

Evidence
- As presented: helps recover from panic or hyperventilation, stops coughing fits, clears brain fog; works
  by slowing breathing and raising CO2 and nitric oxide (2:55).
- My judgement:
  - SOME. The CO2 logic for acute over-breathing is physiologically sound, since symptoms of low CO2
    reverse as it rises. This is a safer version of the discouraged paper-bag advice.
  - Breathing retraining that includes Buteyko has small RCTs in asthma showing better symptoms and less
    reliever use, though not better lung function.
  - CO2-raising training for panic has some trial support.
  - The "brain fog" and long-COVID claims are SPECULATIVE.

Kumbha fit
- NEW practice.
- The current Calm practices are all "slow and long" (resonance, sighing, alternate nostril). This would be
  the first one for "I'm over-breathing right now".
- Excellent for the coach: a short count-down, a one-line instruction, silence.
- Voice: about 9-11 lines: welcome/plan, small-breath rule, tongue and lips, the urge-to-breathe-big line,
  positive signs, why it works (CO2), when to use it, close, 2 tips.
- Count clips: "Hold. Five. Four. Three. Two. One. Let go." The numbers probably already exist.

4. COMFORTABLE-HOLD CHECK  (control pause)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: a personal tracking number. How long can you comfortably pause after a normal exhale? It isn't a
practice; it's a one-minute check.

Structure
- N7 (1:30-1:03) and N8 (0:17-0:42) agree:
  - one normal, silent breath in and out through the nose;
  - pinch the nose and start the timer;
  - stop at the first definite desire to breathe, or the first involuntary movement of the breathing
    muscles;
  - the first breath after should be normal. If you have to gulp, you held too long and the number is
    inflated.
- N8 shows the correction: the guest stopped at 24 s, but his first breaths were slightly fast, so the
  presenter marked it down to 21-22 s.

Cues that made it work
- Stated up front: it doesn't matter what you get; it's feedback, not a test (N7 0:24, N8 0:07).
- This is not a maximum hold (N8 0:35).

Safety
- Seated. No risk at these lengths.

Evidence
- As presented:
  - N7: in asthma, symptoms are typically present below about 20-25 s; the goal is 40 s.
  - N8: below 25 s, breathing-pattern disorder is more likely; above 25 s there's an "89% chance" none is
    present. Higher scores mean less breathlessness during exercise.
- My judgement: SOME as a personal trend number, SPECULATIVE as a diagnosis.
  - A 2008 study (Courtney & Cohen) found breath-hold time correlated poorly with measured end-tidal CO2.
  - The cut-offs and the 89% figure come from the presenters; I couldn't verify them.
  - Use it only to compare yourself with yourself, same time of day, seated, after rest.

Kumbha fit
- NEW tool, not a practice. A "Check" button that runs one hold with record: true and writes the result to
  the practice log the earlier research suggested.
- Shows a 2-week trend with no cut-offs or labels.
- Useful as an optional before/after step in Hold Ladder, Clear Nose and Steady.
- Voice: 3-4 lines (how to, stop-at-first-urge, first-breath-normal, result framing).

5. BREATHE LIGHT  (reduced breathing at a slow pace, plus a sleep version)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: lower the volume of breathing until there's a mild, comfortable air hunger. It's used for calm, and in
X2 as a sleep routine.

Structure
- N9, "light, slow and deep", about 6 minutes of practice:
  - lying on the back, knees bent, hands on the lower ribs;
  - 5.5 s in, 5.5 s out, nose only, timed with bells. Measured "gently breathing in" at 2:25.4, "out" at
    2:30.9, so 5.5 s;
  - from 3:53, shrink the breath until there is a gentle air hunger. If it's too strong, make the breaths
    a little bigger (4:38);
  - 2 minutes of bells only (6:40-8:14), then normal breathing and a check-in.
- X2, the sleep version, 18:46 in all:
  - about 7.5 min of body-scan induction first (a 10-to-1 count-down at 2:16-3:16, then a head-to-toe
    scan 3:18-7:37);
  - then about 11 min of breathe-light from 7:42: slow the inhale by about 30%, breathe so softly you
    barely feel the air;
  - take as many rests as you need (11:13);
  - not timed: "unnecessary to time your breathing" (8:51).
- C5, a cousin: coherent breathing at about 5.5:5.5 with cues to stay "light as a feather" (0:40), a
  body-relaxation pass (3:47-5:15), then 10 min of bells only.

Cues that made it work
- Calibrate by feel, both ways: a small air hunger is the target, and if it's too strong, take a slightly
  bigger breath (N9 3:53-4:43).
- "Just enough air" (N9 3:37, C5 5:37).
- "Make sure you're not holding your breath" (X2 10:28). Light breathing is not breath-holding.
- If you feel constricted, you're overdoing it: rest, breathe normally, then resume (X2 10:40-11:02).
- Name allowed signs: warmth, saliva, a slower mind (C5 5:59, N9 8:25).
- Avoid X2's hypnotic layer: "you feel incredibly sleepy", "every cell relaxing", and the mid-session
  mouth-tape product plug (1:01-1:13).

Safety
- Very low risk.
- Strong air hunger is not the goal. For people with anxiety it can backfire, which is the reason for the
  "make it bigger" escape.
- During a cold, keep it mild (Kumbha already follows this).

Evidence
- As presented:
  - N9: raises CO2, which "improves oxygen delivery to the brain"; reduces anxiety, brain fog and long COVID
    symptoms.
  - X2: oxygen delivery improves, relaxation response, lower heart rate.
- My judgement:
  - SOME for slow breathing in general.
  - SPECULATIVE that the reduced-volume part adds anything for anxiety or sleep.
  - The CO2/Bohr-effect "better oxygen delivery" story is physiology stretched well beyond what a slight
    CO2 rise does at rest.
  - The long-COVID claim is unsupported.

Kumbha fit
- VARIANT.
  - Clear Nose already has a "breathe light" step.
  - Wind-down could gain a 3-minute breathe-light block between 4-7-8 and drift (improvement 3 below).
  - Resonance could offer "light" as a tip.
- A standalone practice would overlap too much.
- Voice: 4-6 lines for the Wind-down block.

6. THE PHYSIOLOGICAL SIGH, ONE TO THREE  (pocket version)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: the fastest in-the-moment reset: one to three sighs, not five minutes.

Structure
- N10: a full inhale through the nose, a second short inhale on top, then a long, extended exhale. Repeat
  1-3 times (2:06-2:48). No guided timing.
- Kumbha's Cyclic Sighing already uses inhale 2.5 s, top-up 1 s, exhale 7 s.

Cues that made it work
- The when: in real time, while life is happening, not on a retreat (0:22).
- One mechanism, one sentence: the second sip re-opens collapsed air sacs, the long exhale offloads CO2.
- A second short mechanism, longer exhales slow the heart (3:02-4:21). Kumbha already says this in
  sigh_heart.

Safety
- Low risk. For someone actively hyperventilating, one or two sighs, then small slow breaths (entry 3),
  rather than repeated big ones.

Evidence
- As presented: 1-3 sighs bring stress down "very, very fast"; found in their own lab and others.
- My judgement:
  - SOME. Spontaneous sighs do act as resetters of breathing.
  - The 2023 RCT tested 5 minutes a day for a month, not the 1-3-sigh acute use.
  - The alveolar re-inflation step is plausible but untested (Kumbha's notes already say so).

Kumbha fit
- VARIANT of Cyclic Sighing: a "Pocket" length of 3 sighs, about 40 s, with the existing close.
- Fits the "choose your length" idea from the earlier app research.
- Voice: 1-2 lines ("Three sighs. That's the whole thing.").

7. RESONANCE PACE FINDER
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: find the slow breathing rate that suits you, rather than assuming 6 a minute.

Structure
- N11: with an ear-clip HRV sensor and a coherence app, 2 minutes at each of three paces: 10-s cycles
  (5:5), 11 s (5.5:5.5), 12 s (6:6). Compare the average coherence scores and pick the highest.
  - His result that day: 3.4 / 4.1 / 3.2. His usual best is 6:6, but that day it was 5.5:5.5 (12:05-12:52).
  - Total about 6 min plus setup.
- N12: most people fall between about 4 and 7 breaths a minute; being within about half a breath a minute
  gets most of the effect; retest every few months.
- N13: first, long mouth exhales to bring the heart down to resting (about 80 s, 1:48-3:11), then match
  the breath to the rise-and-fall of the heart-rate trace. Live graph needed.

Cues that made it work
- Treat it as an experiment with a prediction (N11 3:53).
- Accept day-to-day variation (N11 12:31).
- Slower is not bigger: N4-style soft breaths. Kumbha already has the coh_light line.

Safety
- N12 notes mild rib or upper-belly soreness early on, from unfamiliar mechanics.
- Stop if light-headed (N12 2:40).

Evidence
- As presented: N12 says individual pacing "consistently" beats generic slow breathing; claims better focus,
  recovery and sleep.
- My judgement:
  - SOME for slow breathing raising HRV.
  - The advantage of individual tuning over plain 6 a minute is not well established. My reading of the
    2022 meta-analysis Kumbha already cites is that slow breathing works across rates; check before
    claiming otherwise.
  - Without a heart sensor, Kumbha can only offer a comfort pick, and it should say so.

Kumbha fit: IMPROVEMENT to Resonance (improvement 1 below).

8. COHERENT / SIX-A-MINUTE, COUNTED OR BELLS-ONLY
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: plain slow breathing for HRV and calm.

Structure
- C4: 4 in, 6 out, nose, counted aloud with no gaps for about 3.5 minutes.
  - Numbers are drawn out; C4 calls it seconds, but it measures closer to 3 s and 5 s (genre research).
  - About 80% of the movement from the diaphragm, minimal chest (4:34).
- C5: about 5.5 in and 5.5 out with spoken in/out cues for about 7.5 min, including a body-relaxation pass,
  then 10 minutes of bells only (7:32-18:41).

Cues that made it work
- C4: "low, slow, nose", and don't let the breath get bigger as it gets slower (0:10-0:29).
- C5: tells you to pause the recording any time; then hands over to the bells.

Evidence: SOME to SOLID, as Kumbha's Resonance notes already say. C5 claims saliva and warmth are signs of
"short and long-term benefits", which is overreach.

Kumbha fit
- ALREADY COVERED by Resonance (4:5 then 4:6).
- Two things worth taking: a bells-only extension after the voice stops (C5), and C4's 80% diaphragm
  number as a tip.
- Voice: 1-2 lines.

9. HUMMING SET  (bhramari with ear closure)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: a quick settle using sound. It also warms the voice before speaking.

Structure
- N14, 7 hums in about 5 minutes. Each is a 2-4 s inhale, then a long hum, measured about 15-20 s with
  talk in between.
  - Hums 1-2 are plain (0:57-1:31).
  - Hums 3-4: hum "like a bee", playful (2:08-2:46).
  - Hums 5-7: press the ear flap (tragus) closed while humming, to make the sound louder inside (3:14-4:44).
- N15 finishes with 6 humming breaths, measured about 5 s in and 7 s hum (12-s cycle, 3:49-5:00).
- N16, before a presentation: belly breaths, then wave breaths, then about 6 hums (1:55-3:13), then sit.

Cues that made it work
- The progression gives each hum a new thing to notice: plain, then bee, then ears closed.
- Where do you feel the vibration? (N14 1:57)
- Optional ear-pressing; keep eyes open if you prefer (N14 3:50).

Safety
- None to speak of. Skip pressing the ears with an ear infection.

Evidence
- As presented: humming acts on the vocal cords and the ventral vagus, the "social engagement system"
  (N14 0:12). She reports her smartwatch "body battery" rising (anecdote).
- My judgement:
  - SOLID that humming raises nasal nitric oxide many-fold (Weitzberg & Lundberg 2002). Tide already says
    this.
  - The vagal and polyvagal framing is SPECULATIVE.

Kumbha fit
- ALREADY COVERED: Tide and Clear Nose have hums (HUM = 4 in, 9 hum, 3 pause).
- Two takeaways: the ear-closure progression as a tip for longer hums, and 2-3 hums as the last part of the
  "Ready" practice (entry 10) for people about to speak.
- Voice: 2 lines (bee, ears).

10. READY  (pre-performance reset: exam, presentation, start line)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: about 3-4 minutes before something that matters. Bring the arousal down a notch, keep the head clear,
and finish switched on rather than sleepy. The videos give the pieces; this sequence assembles them.

The pieces, as measured
- N15, a 5-minute morning reset:
  - 6 slow breaths at about 4 in : 8 out (12.0-s cycle, 0:49-2:00);
  - 4 box rounds at about 4-4-4-4 (15-16 s each, 2:27-3:29);
  - 6 humming breaths at about 5 in : 7 hum (11.8 s, 3:49-5:00);
  - mostly silent, with a visual dot pacer.
- C2: the tactical 4:8 is the performance breath. His box close ends on a three-word drive: focus,
  commit, inhale (13:20).
- N10: 1-3 physiological sighs, for use in real time.
- N16: hums before speaking, eyes closed, then "alert yet relaxed" (3:29-3:45).
- N2: on the way to the call.
- N3: running under pressure. He counts 3 steps in, a brief pause, 3 steps out, then adds a mantra
  (1:01-1:22). After a sprint, pause and take one breath to recover (2:30-2:37).

Proposed Kumbha structure, about 3:30, seated or standing
- 0:00 Plan in one line.
- 0:10 Two physiological sighs (about 20 s).
- 0:30 Tactical 4:8, 5 breaths (60 s).
- 1:30 Box 4-4-4-4, 4 rounds (64 s).
- 2:35 Optional, "Stage" mode only: 3 hums of about 8 s.
- 3:05 Back to normal breathing, eyes open, one focus line, "Go."
- Modes:
  - Exam/Desk: no hums.
  - Stage: hums, for the voice.
  - Start line: standing, eyes open, no holds; box swapped for 4:6.

Cues that made it work
- Treat it as a routine you rehearse, not a rescue: C2 says you won't perform it under pressure unless you
  train it.
- End on an action, not a feeling: C2's three-word drive. N15 closes by wishing a good day, which is weaker.
- Keep it short: the earlier app research found 1-3-minute sessions were the most loved thing about
  Breathwrk.

Safety
- Short holds only.
- Not while driving.

Evidence
- As presented: N15 claims to switch the autonomic system from "hyper-sympathetic" to parasympathetic.
- My judgement:
  - SOME for slow breathing lowering pre-performance anxiety.
  - No evidence that this particular combination beats any one of its parts.
  - The honest claim is "takes the edge off; won't make you sleepy at 3-4 minutes".

Kumbha fit
- NEW practice, under a new "Focus" tag, or under Calm.
- Very strong coach fit. It is the briefing before the job.
- Reuses the Box and Sighing patterns and some of their lines.
- Voice: about 8-10 new lines (welcome/plan, three mode-specific intros, focus line, "Go", a tip or two).

11. ATHLETE WARM-UP WITH SHORT MOVING HOLDS
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: switch on before competition, as a breathing layer on top of a normal warm-up.

Structure, N17
- Warm-up jog at low to moderate intensity, nose only, "light, slow, low".
- 2 easy holds while moving: a normal breath in and out, pinch the nose, keep moving for 10-15 paces.
  Then about 1 minute of nasal breathing (0:50-1:10).
- About 5 stronger holds: after a normal exhale, hold and keep moving. As the air hunger rises, raise the
  pace from slow jog to fast jog to run. When it's strong, let go.
- After each strong hold: 6 minimal nose breaths, then 12-18 normal breaths, then repeat (1:12-1:58).
- Total about 10-12 minutes inside the warm-up.
- N18 is the same "easy" stage done as a class: 5 × (10-15 paces held after a normal exhale, then 30-60 s
  of normal nasal breathing) (0:12-3:10). The rule: never go straight into strong holds.

Safety
- On land, on open flat ground, away from traffic, edges, stairs and water.
- Moving holds burn oxygen faster than sitting ones.
- Skip if pregnant, or with heart disease, epilepsy or high blood pressure.

Evidence
- As presented: light breathing raises CO2 so more oxygen reaches the muscles; strong holds release red
  cells from the spleen, raise brain blood flow, and open the nose and lungs (2:12).
- My judgement:
  - The spleen effect is SOLID but small (a few percent more red cells, after several near-maximal holds)
    and fades within minutes. Kumbha's Hold Ladder notes say this.
  - Performance benefit is inconsistent in studies: SPECULATIVE.
  - The CO2/oxygen-delivery point during warm-up is WRONG in degree, since exercise raises CO2 by itself
    anyway.
  - The nose-opening effect of holds is plausible; Clear Nose already uses it.

Kumbha fit
- VARIANT. Best as an optional segment of entry 12 (Walking Holds), or a "Start line" option in Ready.
- I would not ship the strong-hold stage: it conflicts with "hold to the first clear urge".
- Voice: 3-4 lines if folded in.

12. WALKING HOLDS  (dry apnea walk, kept easy)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: practise calm under a rising urge to breathe while moving. It's a freediver's dry drill, and a
mindful walk for everyone else.

Structure, as the videos give it
- N19, the basic walk: 5 minutes at normal pace, breathing slowly through the nose, 3 s in and 3 s out.
  If the air hunger gets too much, slow the walk, not the nose (0:32-1:05).
- N18, easy holds: 5 × (normal breath out, pinch nose, walk 10-15 paces, let go, 30-60 s of normal nasal
  breathing).
- N21, three freediving exercises:
  - Ex. 1 (2:52-4:50): 5 walks each to the first contraction, with 2 minutes of rest between. The rest is
    standing still, tidal breathing through the nose.
  - Ex. 2 (4:53-6:24): walk 20 slow steps (1 step = 1 s) holding, take 2 breaths, repeat. Do 10 minutes,
    building to 15 then 20. Scale down to 15 or 10 steps if hard.
  - Ex. 3 (7:24-8:25): box breathing while walking, steps matched to counts. 4-4-4-4 for 10-15 minutes;
    if easy, 6-6-6-6 next time.
- N20, tables:
  - fixed: 3 × (1:30 walk, 2:00 rest);
  - or building: an easy one, a harder one, then a near-max third (6:45-8:25).
  - Measure by time, not steps or distance. At most twice a week.

Proposed Kumbha structure (Standard, about 12 minutes)
- 3 min nasal walk at 3:3 (N19).
- 6 easy holds after a normal exhale: 15 s, 15, 20, 20, 25, 25 (Gentle 10-15 s; Deeper up to 35 s), each
  followed by 45 s of quiet nasal walking.
- 3 min of box-walk 4-4-4-4 (N21 Ex. 3), then a 1-min easy walk.
- Time-based, since the app can't count steps: one second is about one slow pace (N21 5:05).

Cues that made it work
- Treat the numbers as yours alone; don't compare steps with someone else (N20 5:22-6:11).
- Be honest with yourself: if it was hard, shorten the next one (N21 6:07-7:14). That is pure coach
  register.
- Stop at the first contraction in early sessions. It's an awareness exercise, not a fight (N21 4:19-4:50).
- Reset properly between holds: stand still, breathe normally through the nose (N21 3:59).

Safety (the sources' own rules plus Kumbha's)
- Never with a nose clip, so both airways stay free if you black out (N21 1:02-1:39).
- Grass or sand, not hard ground (N21 1:42-2:02).
- Away from traffic, kerbs, stairs and water.
- Stop if dizzy, light-headed or less coordinated. The source's red line is loss of motor control (2:22),
  which is far too late for Kumbha; stop at the first clear urge.
- Never after fast breathing.
- N21 claims you're "very unlikely" to black out because CO2 will stop you. That is not reliable when
  moving, so don't repeat it.
- Skip if pregnant, or with heart disease, epilepsy or high blood pressure.

Evidence
- As presented:
  - Simulates a dive and trains CO2 tolerance; harder than static holds because CO2 builds faster
    (N21 0:29-1:02).
  - N19 adds a quieter mind.
- My judgement:
  - SOME for breath-hold training lengthening holds (the small apnea-training studies Kumbha cites).
  - SPECULATIVE for any general-health benefit.
  - As a calm-under-discomfort drill it is coherent.

Kumbha fit
- NEW practice under "On the move" or "Long holds".
- Strong coach fit: drills, sets and honest scaling.
- Moderate risk means it needs Gentle as the default and a clear safety gate.
- Voice: about 10-12 lines (plan, ground rules, first-hold line, "stand and reset", honesty line, box-walk
  intro, close, tips).
- Plus say() announcements ("Hold, lungs resting, twenty seconds.").

13. RUNNING BREATH RHYTHM  ("Stride")
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: settle breathing into the stride, as a cue for easy versus hard effort.

Structure
- N25:
  - easy runs 3:3 (3 strides in, 3 out);
  - steady or tempo 2:2;
  - sprint finish 1:1;
  - or odd patterns, 3:2 easy and 2:1 hard, so the exhale lands on alternating feet (0:52-4:11). He notes
    the research behind the odd patterns is disputed.
- N26, nose "gears":
  - easy pace in and out through the nose (walk breaks allowed);
  - faster, in through the nose and out through the mouth;
  - hardest, mouth in and out, but still deep into the belly (1:12-4:08).
- N3: 3 steps in, a brief pause, 3 steps out, plus a mantra, when struggling to keep up.

Proposed Kumbha structure
- 5 min "find the rhythm" with a soft breath tone set from a cadence choice (160, 170 or 180 steps/min).
- 3:3, nose only, then 3:2 for 3 min, then free.
- At 170 spm, 3 steps = 1.06 s, so a 3:2 cycle is 1.76 s (34 breaths/min). The engine handles short
  phases; PUMP already uses 0.42/0.58 s.

Cues that made it work
- Patterns are training wheels for effort: if you can't hold 3:3 on an easy run, you're running too fast
  (N25 1:39).
- Find your own pattern; there's no single right one (N25 4:27).

Safety
- Eyes and ears on traffic; one earbud.
- No holds while running.

Evidence
- As presented:
  - N25: more oxygen in per minute at 2:2. The odd patterns spread impact stress between sides to avoid
    injury, which he flags as disputed.
  - N26: nose breathing gives lower heart rate at the same pace.
- My judgement:
  - Coupling of breath and stride is a real, well-described phenomenon.
  - The injury claim for odd patterns is SPECULATIVE.
  - Nasal-only running lowers ventilation but has no shown performance benefit: SPECULATIVE.

Kumbha fit
- NEW, but lower priority. It needs a cadence setting, and a run needs a different UI (pocket, screen off).
- Coach fit good: short gear calls.
- Voice: about 6-8 lines.

14. INTERVAL RECOVERY BREATH  (between sets)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: get the most out of a 60-90 s rest between hard efforts.

Structure
- N27, the down-shift order (0:31-1:12):
  - in and out through the mouth, slowing the exhale;
  - then nose in, slow relaxed exhale through the mouth;
  - then, as breathlessness falls, nose in and out with a slow, gentle exhale.
  - The theme is "nose, slow, low", with each stage roughly as breathlessness allows.
- N3: you can't sprint forever; pause, and one breath gets you some recovery (2:23-2:37).

Proposed: a 60 s or 90 s timer:
- 0-20 s, mouth, long exhales;
- 20-45 s, nose in, mouth out (4:6-ish);
- 45-90 s, nose only;
- a 10-s warning, then "Go".
- Posture cue: hands on knees.

Evidence
- As presented: slow breathing improves alveolar ventilation; fast shallow mouth breathing wastes air to
  dead space; helps heart-rate recovery.
- My judgement:
  - The dead-space point is SOLID physiology.
  - Whether deliberate slowing speeds recovery between intervals is SOME at best: small studies, mixed.
  - Not from the videos: a small 2019 study (Michaelson et al.) found hands-on-knees beat hands-on-head for
    heart-rate recovery and tidal volume between intervals. Worth using as the posture cue.

Kumbha fit
- VARIANT of entry 15: a "Between sets" mode of a Recover practice, or a 60/90-s repeating timer.
- Voice: 4-5 lines.

15. POST-WORKOUT DOWNSHIFT  ("Cool-down")
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: switch from training to recovery: heart rate down, head quiet.

Structure, N28, measured
- Lie down, neutral spine, feet up with knees and hips at 90 degrees (calves on a bench or chair), eyes
  closed (0:21-0:46).
- Breathe in through the nose, out through the mouth or nose. Counted aloud at about 4 in : 8 out.
  - "Out" cues at 2:24.8, 2:36.6, 2:48.8, 3:00.7 make a 12.0-s cycle (5 a minute).
  - An "in" at 2:56.8 is followed by "out" at 3:00.7, so the inhale is 3.9 s.
- Counted from about 1:00 to 5:00 (about 20 breaths), then sparse counting and silence until 10:45.
- Ends with one deep breath and a send-off (10:45-11:01).
- "I'll lead you for ten minutes, but go as long as you need" (0:16).

Proposed Kumbha structure, about 8 minutes
- 30 s of setup (feet up), then 1 min of free breathing to let the pant settle.
- 4:8 counted, 15 breaths (3 min).
- 4:8 uncounted, tones only, 3 min.
- Check-in.
- A between-sets mode (entry 14) can live in the same practice.

Cues that made it work
- The posture setup is concrete and specific (feet up, 90/90).
- After the counted block the voice disappears.
- One closing line about recovering.
- Skip the source's mumbled filler counting: Parakeet caught many unclear words in it, so it is not a model.

Safety
- Low risk. Get up slowly: blood pressure can dip after slow breathing with the legs raised. Kumbha
  Resonance's "stand up slowly" line already exists.

Evidence
- As presented: "recovery state", getting the most out of the workout.
- My judgement:
  - SOME. Small studies show slow breathing after exercise speeds the return of vagal tone a little.
  - Effects on training adaptation are unknown.
  - Honest claim: helps you come down faster and feel settled.

Kumbha fit
- NEW practice, under a new "Training" tag, or "On the move".
- Excellent coach fit: training vocabulary, short setup and silence.
- Engine needs nothing new.
- Voice: about 7-9 lines (welcome/setup, feet up, why the long exhale, counted-to-quiet handover, a tip,
  check-in, close, getting up slowly).

16. COLD-WATER BREATH  ("Cold": shower or plunge companion)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: get through the first minute of cold without gasping and hyperventilating, then settle. It's control,
not toughness.

Structure, as the videos run it
- C7, a live voice-over of a 3-minute plunge:
  - first, let the breath do what it wants; don't fight mouth breathing or short inhales (1:17);
  - head back on the tub edge (1:32);
  - then lengthen the exhales, audibly or vocalised (1:44-2:03);
  - once in control, full inhale, a hold at the top, ease out (2:03; to drop for Kumbha);
  - then switch to nasal breathing (2:28), then 5 s in : 5 s out, or box (3:02);
  - get out at 3 minutes, slowly (4:02);
  - afterwards, rewarm by moving rather than rushing into a hot shower or sauna (4:30-4:58).
- N29, a cold shower or tub, about 3 min:
  - a big nasal breath before stepping in, exhaling through the mouth as you get in (0:31);
  - 6 rounds of 5 in through the nose : 5 out through the mouth (0:45-1:37);
  - then about 3 rounds of 5-5-5-5 box (1:42-2:47);
  - then an "optional plunge on the exhale", head under, or face wet in a shower (2:47). Drop for Kumbha.
- N30: mostly intention-setting before the bath, "slow inhale, even slower exhale", then sitting in. Little
  structure.

Proposed Kumbha structure: shower-first, about 4 minutes, all of it on audio from outside the shower
- 45 s on land: 6 breaths at 4:6, nose in, mouth out.
- Step in or turn to cold on an exhale.
- 0:00-0:45 in the cold:
  - let the gasp happen; then long exhales through pursed lips or a hum;
  - a "longer out than in" cue every few breaths.
- 0:45-2:00: 5 in through the nose : 5 out through the mouth, then nose only.
- At 2:00 (Gentle 1:00, Deeper 3:00): "Out. Slowly."
- 1 min of rewarm cues: move, dry off, no hot shower straight away.
- No holds at any point in the water, and no fast breathing before or during.

Cues that made it work
- Permission first: the gasp is normal; don't fight it for the first breaths (C7 1:17).
- Then one job: make the exhale longer (C7 1:44, N29 0:53).
- Rest the head; relax the shoulders (C7 1:32).
- A fixed exit time, announced (C7 4:02).
- The rewarm advice is concrete and a little surprising (C7 4:30).
- The coach voice suits it: terse, calm, a bit dry.

Safety (this is where Kumbha would add real value over the sources)
- Never breath-holds or fast breathing in or before cold water. Both sources add holds in the water, and N29
  suggests going under on an exhale. Cold shock shortens breath-holds and triggers an involuntary gasp,
  which in open water is how people drown.
- Drownings have been reported after hyperventilation-plus-hold practice in water. Say so plainly.
- Shower or a tub you can step out of. Never alone in open water.
- Skip it with heart disease, high blood pressure, Raynaud's, or if pregnant.
- Get out if you're shivering hard or getting clumsy, or if fingers go white or numb.

Evidence
- As presented:
  - C7: dopamine you can "literally feel", "anti-inflammatory properties".
  - N30: white blood cells increase; reprogram the subconscious.
  - All WRONG or SPECULATIVE as stated.
- My judgement:
  - SOLID that the cold-shock response (gasp, hyperventilation, heart-rate spike) peaks in the first minute
    and fades over a few minutes, and that it shrinks with repeated short exposures (Tipton's cold-shock
    research).
  - SOME that deliberately slowing the breath shortens that phase.
  - Mood benefits of cold showers come from small, mixed studies.

Kumbha fit
- NEW practice under "Energy", or a new "Cold" tag.
- Kumbha's current safety line ("never in or near water, in the bath") would need a scoped exception: no
  holds, no fast breathing, shower first.
- Coach fit is excellent.
- Voice: about 10-12 lines (plan, step-in exhale, gasp-is-normal, long-exhale, rhythm, nose, exit, rewarm,
  safety, two tips).

17. PRESSURE BREATHING AND EXHALE-ON-EFFORT  (hills, stairs, altitude)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Goal: keep breathlessness under control on steep climbs, stairs, or above about 3,000 m.

Structure
- N32, mountaineering:
  - above about 3,000 m, match breathing to steps;
  - before a steep or vertical section, 2-3 "pressure breaths": a full deep inhale, then a forceful exhale
    against pursed lips that takes its time to empty fully (0:49-1:22);
  - if too out of breath for that, first slow down with mouth breathing, then resume (1:28-2:00).
- N34, pulmonary rehab:
  - inhale through the nose while standing still at the bottom of the stairs;
  - exhale (pursed lips) as you step up;
  - never hold the breath and rush (0:12-1:20).
- N33: a trek-prep pranayama routine:
  - belly breathing;
  - alternate-nostril with a short hold, 3-count in, 5-15 rounds;
  - kapalabhati, 10-30 forceful exhales, with contraindications listed (gastric ulcers, high or low blood
    pressure, pregnancy, recent abdominal surgery).

Proposed Kumbha structure
- "Uphill", a short on-the-move practice:
  - 1 min of nasal walking;
  - then a pattern of 1 breath in over 1 step, out over 2 steps with pursed lips, on the climb;
  - every 2 minutes, 2 pressure breaths;
  - stop-and-reset on any spike.
- Alternatively, a "stairs" tip set inside Window Seat's travel family.

Cues that made it work
- Exhale on the effort, inhale at rest (N34).
- Slowing down is part of the technique, not a failure (N32 1:53, N34 0:41).
- The rest step (lock the lower knee each step) is the mechanical partner, but those videos give no breath
  timings.

Safety
- None specific for healthy people.
- For anyone with lung or heart disease, this is a rehab tool to learn with their team.
- Breathing does not prevent altitude sickness: ascend slowly; descend if symptoms worsen.

Evidence
- As presented:
  - N33: belly breathing "increases oxygen supply", kapalabhati raises body temperature and lung capacity,
    exhales "push out toxins". All WRONG.
  - N32: pressure breathing helps "a lot" (anecdote).
- My judgement:
  - SOLID that pursed-lip breathing eases breathlessness in COPD.
  - The pressure-breathing rationale at altitude is plausible (higher airway pressure keeps small airways
    open, as in PEEP); only small field data, so SPECULATIVE to SOME.
  - No breathing technique is shown to prevent acute mountain sickness.

Kumbha fit
- NEW but niche, under "On the move".
- Low priority, unless hikers are an audience.
- Voice: about 6-8 lines.

18. CO2 TABLE  (follow-along) AND A FAIRER ALTERNATIVE
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Structure, C3, measured
- 6 holds of 45 s after a slow inhale to about 80%.
- Rests start at 1:30 and drop by 15 s each time: 1:30, 1:15, 1:00, 0:45, 0:30, 0:15.
- An app voice gives "Coming up, hold in 15", exhale that last breath, inhale to 80%, a 3-2-1 count-down,
  "Hold" (e.g. 2:33.9-2:49.4).
- Measured holds: 44.2, 43.4, 44.3, 44.5 s.
- "Recover" twice at the end of each hold.
- After the last hold, "extended recovery", then the option of 2 minutes of recovery breathing and a
  max-hold test (11:10-11:27).
- If 45 s is too long, use 15 or 30 s.

The critique, N24
- Because rests shrink, later holds start with CO2 already high. So the table mostly teaches fighting
  discomfort, not relaxing into the early stages (4:41-5:27).
- The coach's alternative: train the early stage with holds that all start from the same CO2. For
  top-stage training, take just one or two breaths between holds (6:31-6:50). The latter is an advanced
  method not for an app.
- Any hold trains CO2 tolerance; shrinking rests aren't required (7:29-8:14).

Kumbha fit
- ALREADY COVERED by Hold Ladder (6 holds, rests 1:30 to 0:15, intensities 25/45/60 s).
- C3 has three cues Kumbha lacks: the 15-s heads-up, the 80% inhale, and the optional max-hold test.
- N24 suggests a "Steady rests" option.
- See improvement 2 below.
- Evidence: SOME for tables lengthening holds (as Kumbha's notes already say). The critique is coaching
  opinion, but physiologically coherent.

19. O2 TABLE  (rising holds, fixed rests)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Structure
- N22: test your max hold.
- Holds then rise towards 90-95% of it, in steps of 5 s (max under 1:00), 10 s (1:30-2:00), or 15 s
  (over 2:00). His example for a 1:00 max is 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55 s.
- Rest is always 2 minutes of "pendulum" breathing, 5 in : 5 out.
- 4-8 rounds. Optionally add a 100% max at the end. Retest weekly and re-scale (0:36-3:30).
- N23: hypoxic training only works if it is "mildly hypoxic"; build up gradually; about twice a week. He
  trains in the pool with a buddy.

Safety
- O2 tables deliberately approach low-oxygen levels. Blackout risk is real even sitting, and higher than in
  CO2 tables.
- Dry, lying down, never alone, never in water, never after fast breathing.

Evidence
- As presented: builds tolerance to low oxygen.
- My judgement: SOME that hold time improves with training. The adaptation is mostly tolerance and the
  diving response.

Kumbha fit
- SKIP for now, or offer only as a Hold Ladder variant capped at about 60-70% of a comfortable hold.
- Its whole point (near-maximal holds) conflicts with Kumbha's "first clear urge" principle and its solo,
  at-home use.
- The fixed 2-minute rest is worth borrowing (improvement 2).

20. POWER-BREATHING ROUNDS  (deep cyclic breathing, empty hold, 15-s recovery hold), incl. a sleep version
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Structure, measured
- C1, 3 rounds. Each round:
  - about 30-35 full in/out breaths over about 105-110 s (about 3.6 s each);
  - "10 more", "5 more", "last one" milestones;
  - an empty hold of about 23 s, 60 s, then about 90 s in round 3 (8:16-9:47, "30 seconds left" at 9:18);
  - a full recovery breath held 15 s.
- C6, 4 rounds of slower deep breathing (about 1.5-2 min each). Measured empty holds of about 59 s, 91 s,
  121 s and 164 s, each with a 15-s recovery hold. Mostly silent.
- X1, the sleep version, 3 rounds:
  - 2-count in through the nose, 2 out through the mouth, for about 105 s (about 25 breaths);
  - a short top hold of 6-10 s, exhale, an empty hold of about 50-55 s, a recovery hold of 13-21 s;
  - "halfway", "three more, two, last one" countdowns.

Evidence: covered in Kumbha's Tide and Fire Wave notes (adrenaline rise in small studies; SpO2 drops in the
holds).

Kumbha fit
- ALREADY COVERED by Tide (kapalabhati, empty holds 45/60/75 s, full holds) and Fire Wave.
- I would not add X1 as a sleep practice: deep cyclic breathing with holds is arousing, which is the wrong
  direction at bedtime.
- One cue worth keeping: C1's "if you need to breathe before the cue, that's okay" (5:28).
- Voice: 0.

21. "TUMMO"-STYLE ROUNDS  (as taught on a large yoga channel)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Structure, N31, 3 rounds
- Deep belly-then-chest inhales with forceful pursed-lip exhales, as if throwing the air at your hand.
  The video says 21 breaths per round; it measures as about 25-30 s of breathing in round 1.
- Then a full inhale and hold: 30 s, 45 s, then 60 s with a root lock in round 3.
- A slow, controlled pursed-lip exhale.
- 30 s of seated stillness, visualising a flame at the base of the spine.
- About 7 minutes in all (2:16-8:01).

Evidence
- As presented: "detoxes", improves metabolism, burns fat, purifies the "pranic body", "five times more
  effective" with the lock. All WRONG or SPECULATIVE.
- My judgement: a 2013 study of Tibetan practitioners (Kozhevnikov et al.) found the forceful "vase"
  breathing raised core temperature, and the visualisation helped sustain it. SOME, for heat only. The
  video's version is closer to Fire Wave than to that.

Kumbha fit
- ALREADY COVERED by Fire Wave (full-lung holds with bandhas, 15/30/45 s).
- Two small borrowings: the controlled pursed-lip release after the hold, and 30 s of stillness between
  rounds.
- Voice: 1-2 lines.

22. COOLING BREATH  (sitali)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Structure, N35, 5-10 cycles
- Lips in an "O", tongue curled and stuck out.
- Inhale through the tongue like sipping through a straw, with a hiss.
- Close the mouth, chin to chest (chin lock), hold 5 s.
- Lift the chin and exhale slowly through the nose (0:25-1:10).
- About 15 s per cycle, so 1.5-2.5 minutes.

Evidence
- As presented: cools the body, soothes eyes and ears, promotes liver and spleen, improves digestion,
  reduces thirst. Mostly WRONG.
- My judgement: the evaporative cooling of the incoming air is real but tiny. It's a SPECULATIVE comfort
  practice for a hot day. About a fifth or more of people can't curl the tongue; the usual alternative is
  sipping air through the teeth (sitkari).

Kumbha fit
- SKIP as a full practice. At most a 2-minute "hot day" variant in Calm.
- The coach voice can carry it ("Curl the tongue. If you can't, teeth together, sip through them."), but
  the evidence is thin.
- Voice: 4-5 lines.

23. 4-7-8  (counted)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Structure, X3
- 4 in through the nose, 7 hold, 8 out through the mouth. Counts at about 1.3-1.6 s each, so a real cycle
  of about 27-28 s.
- About 20 cycles in 10 minutes, then free breathing.

Kumbha fit
- ALREADY COVERED by Wind-down (4 rounds).
- Nothing new except confirmation that slow counting reads as calm.

24. LONG-INHALE "AWAKE" BREATH FOR FLYING
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Structure, V13: 6 s in through the nose, 2 s out through the mouth.

Evidence
- As presented: cabin pressure lowers blood oxygen, which makes you sleepy. Long exhales lower blood oxygen,
  long inhales raise it and "activate the energizing branch".
- My judgement: WRONG. Inhale length doesn't raise saturation in any meaningful way, and fast,
  inhale-heavy breathing mostly lowers CO2.

Kumbha fit
- SKIP. It also contradicts Window Seat's reasoning (cabin air, no over-breathing).

=====================================================================================================
RANKED: TOP 5 NEW PRACTICES TO ADD
=====================================================================================================
1. BOX (entry 1)
   - Tactical 4:8 opener, then box 4-4-4-4 into 5-5-5-5; about 7 min, with Gentle/Deeper/No holds.
   - Why first: the most-searched calm technique missing from the library, low risk, a native fit for the
     ex-military coach (drills, ratios, benchmark close), and the engine already supports it.
   - About 12-14 lines.
2. STEADY (entries 3 + 2)
   - 10-12 small holds of 5 s with 12-s rests, then a 3:6 long-exhale ladder; about 7 min.
   - Why: fills the "I'm over-breathing right now" gap; the sighing and resonance practices go the other
     way.
   - Reuses Clear Nose's pinch step.
   - About 10-12 lines.
3. READY (entry 10)
   - 2 sighs, tactical 4:8 ×5, box ×4, optional hums, "Go"; 3-4 min.
   - Modes: Desk, Stage, Start line.
   - Why: a short, high-use practice (exams, talks, start lines) that recombines patterns Kumbha already
     has; the coach briefing tone is ideal.
   - About 8-10 lines.
4. COLD (entry 16)
   - Shower-first cold companion: let the gasp happen, long exhales, 5:5, exit on time, rewarm by moving;
     no holds in water.
   - Why: cold exposure is hugely popular, and the YouTube versions add holds and head-dunks. An honest,
     safety-led version is a real differentiator. It needs a scoped change to the water rule.
   - About 10-12 lines.
5. COOL-DOWN (entries 15 + 14)
   - Feet up 90/90, 4:8 counted for 3 min, then 3 min of tones only; plus a 60/90-s "between sets" mode.
   - Why: a training audience gets a reason to open Kumbha after every session; low risk; nothing new in
     the engine.
   - About 9-11 lines across both modes.

Runners-up:
- Walking Holds (entry 12): good coach fit, but moderate risk and needs a careful safety gate.
- Stride (entry 13): needs a cadence setting and a running UI.
- Comfortable-hold check (entry 4): a tool rather than a practice, but cheap and it feeds the practice log.

=====================================================================================================
THREE IMPROVEMENTS TO EXISTING PRACTICES
=====================================================================================================
1. RESONANCE: "Find your pace" plus a bells-only extension (entries 7 and 8)
   - An optional first-run session: 2 minutes each at 6.5, 6, 5.5 and 5 breaths a minute (cycles of 9.2,
     10, 10.9 and 12 s, keeping the 2:3 in/out split). Then a tap: "Which felt easiest?" Store it and use
     it as Resonance's main pace.
   - Say plainly that without a heart sensor this is a comfort pick, that most people land between 4.5
     and 6.5, that 6 is a good default, and that the best rate can shift day to day (N11 saw this).
   - Add "+5 / +10 min, bells only" after the close, as in C5.
   - About 4 lines.
2. HOLD LADDER: three cues from C3, and a fairer table from N24 and N22
   - A heads-up 15 s before each hold, a count-in, and "Recover." as a one-word call.
   - "Breathe in to about 80%" instead of "comfortably full".
   - An optional comfortable-hold check (entry 4) or easy max-hold test, after 2 minutes of recovery
     breathing, logged for the trend.
   - A "Steady rests" option: the same holds, but a constant 2:00 rest (5:5 breathing), so every hold
     starts from the same CO2. Kinder for beginners, and it answers the critique.
   - About 5-6 lines.
3. WIND-DOWN: a breathe-light block before the drift (entry 5)
   - After the four 4-7-8 rounds, 3 minutes of breathe-light: nose only, make each breath a little
     smaller until there's a faint air hunger. If it gets strong, take a slightly bigger breath. Rest any
     time.
   - Then the existing drift.
   - Keep X2's calibration cues and drop its hypnotic "you feel sleepy" layer.
   - Evidence for sleep is weak, so phrase it as "quiet, small breathing", not a sleep cure.
   - About 4-5 lines.
- Honourable mention: Cyclic Sighing's "Pocket" length of 3 sighs (entry 6). It costs 1-2 lines and fits
  the choose-your-length idea from the app research.
